Ender 3 V2 Rebuild

A cheap printer I took apart down to the base plate and rebuilt: squared by hand, rewired, converted to direct drive and running a Klipper configuration I wrote myself.

Role
Solo project
Timeline
Dec 2021 - ongoing

Klipper · Marlin · Orbiter v2 · BLTouch · Orca Slicer
Status
Running · about 1,250 h printed
Constraint
Lives in the living room

Almost everything I print is a part for another project.

I bought the printer in December 2021. It is not a project on its own shelf: nearly everything that comes off it is a bracket, mount or housing I designed in CAD for something else. That is what made the machine itself worth engineering. A printer that needs supervision is not a tool, and I wanted a tool.

It is also the machine behind the weekly CAD, 3D-printing and robotics courses I lead, which meant I had to actually understand why it behaved the way it did.

After a year of barely using it, every layer came out slightly different.

Towards the end of 2022 I started printing seriously again, and the results were wrong in a way I could see but not explain: each layer stuck out a little differently from the one below it, so the walls came out uneven all the way up the part.

I assumed the cause was mechanical. Uneven layers look like a geometry problem, and this is a printer assembled from a kit by a fifteen-year-old, so a crooked frame was an entirely believable explanation.

So I took the whole printer apart, down to the base plate.

In mid-2023 I stripped it completely - not a partial teardown, but everything removed until only the base plate was left - and rebuilt it with a caliper and an engineer's square, checking that things were actually at right angles instead of assuming it.

Several parts changed on the way back together. The bed springs were replaced with solid spacers, so the bed stops drifting out of level between prints. The glass plate became a PEI sheet. I bought heatbed insulation and cut it to size. I also moved the original extruder stepper to the Y axis, because its datasheet gave it more torque than the motor that had been sitting there.

The problem was never the frame. It was flow.

I had changed the extruder twice - the plastic Bowden extruder for an aluminium one, and later the whole thing for an Orbiter v2 direct drive - and I had not properly recalibrated the extrusion afterwards. The machine was pushing an inconsistent amount of filament, and that was what I had been seeing on every layer.

The teardown did not find the bug. It did fix a list of things I did not know were wrong, and it taught me the more useful lesson: when the hardware changes, recalibrate before forming a theory. Testing the cheap hypothesis first would have cost an evening instead of a summer.

The slicer profiles came out of the same period. Most of the values in my Orca profiles are trial and error, with a temperature tower and flow calibration underneath them to keep the guessing bounded.

The wiring was the part that actually worried me.

The stock cables arrived with their conductor ends tinned - dipped in solder - and then clamped into screw terminals. Solder creeps under sustained pressure, so the joint slowly loosens, the contact resistance rises and the terminal heats. I met the failure first-hand: a badly seated connection sparked.

I re-terminated the screw connections with crimped ferrules and went through the grounding while everything was open. Two components were replaced along the way: a dead thermistor, whose connector I soldered onto new wires, and a failed part-cooling fan. The loose cables that were now hanging around got a holder I designed and printed, mounted in the spot the original Bowden extruder had occupied.

It had to be quiet, because it lives in the living room.

That was the only space available, which turned noise into a real requirement rather than a nice extra. The mainboard fan came off entirely. Klipper needs a Raspberry Pi anyway, so its replacement is a 5 V fan running off the Pi's 3.3 V rail: deliberately undervolted, so it turns slowly and I cannot hear it. The power supply fan was replaced with a 120 mm Noctua, and I reprinted the power supply enclosure on the printer itself before fitting it.

Quieter cooling is less cooling, so the airflow I had removed had to come back passively. I took out the doubled metal plate in front of the power supply so that it could actually breathe, and raised the whole printer on printed risers to open up the space underneath it.

Klipper, before there was a configuration for this printer.

I started on mriscoc's Professional Firmware, a Marlin fork for this machine, and moved to Klipper around 2022. At that point there was no ready-made Ender 3 V2 configuration to copy, so I wrote mine from the Klipper documentation and corrected it by trial and error until the printer behaved. The mainboard had been swapped for a Creality 4.2.7 by then, and bed levelling runs on a BLTouch with adaptive meshing, so the mesh is measured across the area the print actually occupies.

A set of macros for automating parts of a print run is still unfinished, which is the honest state of it.

Two upgrades I tested and did not keep.

Input shaping and pressure advance are the two things every guide tells you to enable. I tried both and kept neither. My print speeds are low enough that input shaping had almost nothing to correct, and my pressure advance test prints were indistinguishable from the ones printed with no pressure advance at all.

Working out which parameters do nothing on a particular machine turned out to be about as valuable as finding the ones that do. It is the difference between tuning a printer and copying someone else's tune.

Where it stands now.

The machine has been running since December 2021 and still is. Parts I design come off it fitting properly the first time, with visibly cleaner walls than before the rebuild, and failed prints are rare. Its speed ceiling is the original hotend: the maximum rate at which it can melt filament, rather than anything in the motion system, is what stops me going faster.

Most of my printing now happens on a preassembled Prusa MK4S, simply because it is faster. That printer also arrived with a dimensional problem of its own - so I took it apart too, and fixed it. Being able to do that to a machine I had not built is the clearest thing the Ender gave me.
